Investigation 001 / Infrastructure

Cable Drawer Inquiry

Drawer 3 contains enough cable to connect several eras of personal computing, but not always to one another.

Resolved
retention authorized

Executive summary

A small drawer with an unusually long memory

Drawer 3 was opened after a USB-C cable could not be found despite a reasonable belief that at least four were present. The review documented 40 cable specimens, three adapters, and one unidentified power supply. One of the four USB-C cables was eventually found in service elsewhere.

The collection is not random. It is a compressed history of machines once owned, borrowed, repaired, or prepared for. Every connector marks a period when a certain port seemed permanent. VGA belonged to monitors that could be made to work by tightening two little screws. Mini-USB belonged to a camera whose battery door no longer closes. The printer cable remains attached, in memory if not in fact, to the sound of an inkjet cleaning its heads at the worst possible moment.

The practical question was which cables still deserved space. The less practical question, introduced almost immediately, was whether disposal might leave the Department unprepared for a future in which somebody urgently needs to connect a 2007 scanner.

Cable Forward 2026 has converted a drawer problem into an infrastructure opportunity. All future references to “sorting things out” should use the approved term portfolio rationalization.

Finding A / Inventory

The collection is diverse, duplicated, and mostly explicable

Specimens were grouped by the job they once performed rather than by the device they happened to accompany. This prevents the usual category of “cable for something” from becoming a permanent classification.

USB and Ethernet appear in multiples because they are still routinely lent, misplaced, routed badly, or found to be the wrong length. Duplicates here represent actual resilience. The two HDMI leads also survive scrutiny. By contrast, the single PS/2 extension preserves access to no keyboard currently owned and extends it farther away from no computer currently owned.

A collection with VGA and DisplayPort demonstrates end-to-end capability. It is like owning both a canoe and a space programme: the Department can meet stakeholders wherever they are.

Finding B / Legacy systems

Obsolescence is uneven because memory is not a retention policy

The audio and video lot produced the strongest case for selective release. SCART is magnificent in the way a connector can be magnificent: enormous, directional only after close inspection, and capable of loosening under its own confidence. It recalls the television behind which it was once installed, where every adjustment required moving a cabinet and making peace with dust.

Yet the composite RCA set has earned its place twice in recent years: once for a borrowed game console and once for diagnosing an old video deck. The 3.5 mm leads are still ordinary tools. Coaxial remains attached to a plausible household use. The SCART lead survives for a different reason. It belongs with one particular old console, and separating them would convert a working object into two incomplete objects stored in different places.

This is where a purely numerical policy fails. Some old standards remain live because the old device remains live. Others are only souvenirs disguised as contingencies. The distinction is not age; it is whether the cable completes something real.

Leadership rejects the term “obsolete.” Approved doctrine is strategic optionality: every cable is awaiting a future compatibility event. Disposal reduces readiness by one cable per cable.

Finding C / Retention

A cable should either work now, complete a kept device, or leave

The internal storage and power lots were tested against a simple rule. SATA remains current enough to keep. One IDE ribbon cable remains with the last compatible drive until its contents are reviewed; the duplicate does not acquire additional historical importance by being equally flat.

  1. Retain active standards in quantities supported by actual simultaneous use.
  2. Retain complete sets when a cable is necessary to operate a specific device that is itself being kept.
  3. Label proprietary power supplies with the device family before institutional memory fails.
  4. Release duplicates and unsupported standards unless a named, testable use exists.

Under that rule, four specimens were selected for disposal: one IDE ribbon, the PS/2 extension, an excess Micro-USB lead, and the telephone cable. Two unidentifiable adapters were separated for electronic recycling. General mains leads, IEC C13 and C7 figure-eight cords, the labelled laptop barrel charger, SATA, and the remaining data cables were returned in loose coils, which the Clerk recognizes as an incomplete operational improvement.

Clerk’s correction: Before collection, the telephone lead was recovered on the basis that a future modem might require it. The IDE ribbon was also recovered, although no future drive was named. Net reduction: two cables and two adapters, not four cables as stated in the closing briefing.

Drawer capacity liberated: 17%.

Method: visual estimate made while the drawer was open. Seventeen percent is almost a fifth, which is basically a quarter in momentum terms.

Final ruling / DPA-001

Selective retention authorized

Drawer 3 may continue as a working cable archive, subject to three conditions: every retained cable must have a named use; device-specific cables must be stored with or labelled for that device; and speculative future compatibility alone does not constitute a use.

The Department finds that careful disposal can preserve memory better than indiscriminate keeping. A smaller collection makes each surviving connection easier to see.

Approved with amendments. Strategic optionality remains official policy until the next drawer review, currently outside staffing capacity.
